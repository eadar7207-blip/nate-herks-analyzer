## [Everything Alex Hormozi Said About AI in 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dlkZr61G6vA)
*November 11 at 12:44 PM*

WHAT IT'S ABOUT

This video breaks down everything Alex Hormozi (a well-known business guy) has said about AI over the past year, pulling from over 200 of his videos and posts. Nate made it to show a surprising twist: Hormozi loves AI and uses it everywhere, but he just cut his team's AI spending budget to zero. The big lesson is that spending money on AI tools doesn't help unless you point that AI at the thing actually holding your business back.

THE KEY POINTS

- Hormozi is a huge fan of AI — he says it takes up about half of his business thinking. **He's not anti-AI; he's anti-wasting money on AI.**

- For their book launch, his team built five AI agents (software robots that answer customer questions automatically) that handled 120,000 support requests and solved 90% without any human. **Most of those questions were simple, repetitive stuff a bot handles easily.**

- His media team tripled their content output without hiring anyone, because AI prompts (the written instructions you give an AI) now do 30–50% of the work. **One person can now do the work of three, five, or even ten people.**

- His way of thinking about jobs changed: instead of "I need to hire an editor," he asks "what five things does an editor actually do?" and turns each one into a workflow (a step-by-step repeatable process). **Break a job into tasks, then automate each task.**

- He uses AI to help write but doesn't let it write for him — tweeting went from 90 minutes a day to 90 minutes a week. **AI generates ideas; he refines them so they still sound like him.**

- He feeds his recorded meetings and calendar to AI to find interesting real stories for content, instead of making things up.

- On sales: one text-based AI agent sells about 200 units a month, but humans still beat AI on phone calls even after two years of voice AI development. **AI wins at text; humans still win at talking.**

- The $350,000 mistake: a business paid 11 assistants $11,000 a month to clean data (work that was already going fine), then spent $350,000 building AI to replace them — on a task that wasn't even holding the business back. **Don't automate something that doesn't matter.**

- He cut his own team's AI budget because revenue stayed flat while AI usage soared, meaning people were using AI on projects that don't move the needle. **Tokens (the units AI companies charge you for) cost money whether or not the work was useful.**

- He won't fire people just because of AI — his companies keep hiring. But he does say owners should "raise the bar" and keep only people who can meet the new standard.

THE METHOD OR FRAMEWORK

Hormozi's test for whether AI is actually worth it comes down to three questions you ask yourself:

1. **Since I started using AI, am I getting more views?**
2. **Am I getting more leads (potential customers)?**
3. **Am I getting more money?**

If your AI bill keeps growing but these three numbers don't, you're using AI on the wrong things. The fix: find your "true limit" — the one real bottleneck holding your business back (usually it's leads, sales, or delivery) — and point your AI straight at that. His other rule for deciding whether to automate a task: first ask whether the task should even exist. Sometimes the smartest move is to kill a task, not automate it. And his career advice to his team sums up the whole mindset: "Show how little you can use and how much you can get from it."

HOW THIS APPLIES TO AI REAL ESTATE

This is gold for an AI real estate consulting business because your clients (real estate agents and brokerages) will want you to "add AI" everywhere, and your job is to stop them from making the $350,000 mistake. Point every AI project at one measurable number — usually more leads, more appointments booked, or faster deal closing. Concrete example: a brokerage asks you to build a fancy AI tool that organizes their old property photos. That might be fun, but it won't grow their business. Instead, you look at where they actually lose money and find that leads from their website sit for hours before anyone replies. So you build a text-based AI agent that instantly answers new leads 24/7, qualifies them, and books showings — just like Hormozi's text agent that sells 200 units a month. You keep humans on the phone calls, since those still convert better. Then you prove your value by showing the agent's lead response time dropped and booked showings went up, which directly ties AI spending to more money.

ACTION STEP THIS WEEK

Pick one current or potential real estate client and run Hormozi's three-question test on their business. Specifically: (1) Write down their single biggest bottleneck — is it getting leads, converting leads into appointments, or closing deals? (2) Identify one AI workflow that attacks exactly that bottleneck (for example, an instant text-response agent if slow lead follow-up is the problem). (3) Define the one number you'll measure to prove it worked (like "response time under 2 minutes" or "20% more showings booked"). Put all three in writing and use it as your pitch.

BEST QUOTE

"If you use AI all day and don't make more money, it means you have the same problem you had before AI. You are doing things that don't matter. Only this time it costs you tokens."

## [Would you pick an AI co-founder or a human one?](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/9qvqlSxgzgk)

WHAT IT'S ABOUT

The point of the video is to get a quick read on how people feel about trusting AI as a true partner right now, versus just a helpful tool.

THE KEY POINTS

- **Almost everyone picked a human co-founder** — the common answer was that a real person is still the better choice today.
- Many people added the phrase "for now," meaning **they think AI could become a real co-founder soon, maybe within a year.**
- Several people want the best of both worlds: **a human partner who is also skilled at using AI**, so you get the human touch plus AI power.
- A few people mentioned that AI should be used as an **assistant or helper**, not as the main partner.
- One person referenced "Opus 5.5" (a newer, more advanced version of an AI model) as a sign that **AI is improving fast and getting close to being partner-worthy.**
- A couple of people leaned toward AI because **they feel they'd actually use it more and it challenges them by answering their questions.**
- The overall mood: **people value the human connection in business, but they see the gap between humans and AI closing quickly.**

THE METHOD OR FRAMEWORK

There's no real framework in this video — it's just a quick opinion poll asking one question. So there's nothing to break down step by step here.

HOW THIS APPLIES TO AI REAL ESTATE

This tells you something useful for an AI real estate consulting business: people still want the human relationship, but they're warming up fast to AI as a partner or assistant. That's exactly the sweet spot your business lives in. For example, imagine a real estate agent who's overwhelmed with leads. They don't want to be replaced by a robot, but they'd love an AI assistant that answers new buyer questions at 2 a.m., books showings automatically, and follows up with cold leads. You're not selling "replace yourself with AI" — you're selling "a human agent who is an AI pro," which is literally the answer people called the best one in this video. Position your consulting that way: you help agents stay human with clients while AI handles the repetitive back-end work.

Record a 60-second demo of it working and send it to three local agents this week.

BEST QUOTE

"I want a human co-founder who is an AI pro."

## [Do people say please to AI?](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/16lpGeMZlzY)
*November 06 at 02:01 PM*

WHAT IT'S ABOUT

This is a short, fun street-interview style video where Nate asks a bunch of people one simple question: do you say "please" and "thank you" when you talk to artificial intelligence (AI), meaning computer programs like ChatGPT that can chat with you? There's no deep lesson here — it's a lighthearted pulse-check on how regular people feel about being polite to machines, and the mix of reasons they give is pretty funny and revealing.

THE KEY POINTS

- **Most people said they DO say "please" and "thank you" to AI**, even though they know it's just software and not a real person.
- Some people admitted they're polite mostly out of **habit — being nice is just automatic for them**, so they do it with machines too.
- A few people were half-joking that they're polite because **they're worried about a future "AI uprising" and want to be on the robots' good side** if machines ever get too powerful.
- One person pointed out that being polite actually **gets you better answers from the AI**, suggesting kindness isn't just nice — it can be useful.
- A handful of people flat-out said no — **they skip the manners entirely and just give commands**, and one even felt a little guilty about it.
- Someone mentioned the "extra costs" angle — that being wordy with AI (adding "please," etc.) **wastes resources like water and energy**, since running these programs uses real power behind the scenes.

THE METHOD OR FRAMEWORK

There's no real system or framework in this video — it's just a casual opinion poll on the street, so there's nothing step-by-step to break down.

HOW THIS APPLIES TO AI REAL ESTATE

Even though this clip is playful, there's a real nugget for an AI real estate consulting business: how you talk to AI affects the results you get. One person said politeness gave them "much better answers." That's a clue about something called prompting — the way you write instructions to an AI to get what you want. For example, if you're building an AI assistant that answers questions for home buyers, the tone and clarity of your instructions matter. A rushed, sloppy command like "list houses" might give a thin answer, while a clear, well-structured request — "Please give me five three-bedroom homes under $400,000 in this zip code, with the price and square footage for each" — gives the AI more to work with and produces a cleaner result. So the lesson for your business is: teach clients that talking to AI like a thoughtful human, with full context, gets sharper, more reliable answers.

ACTION STEP THIS WEEK

Run a simple test. Pick one task you'd give an AI tool like ChatGPT — for example, writing a property listing description. Type the request two ways: first as a blunt command ("write a listing for a 3-bed house"), then as a polite, detailed request ("Please write a warm listing for a 3-bedroom, 2-bath house with a renovated kitchen and big backyard, aimed at young families"). Compare the two answers side by side and note which one is more usable. This shows you firsthand how wording changes output — a core skill for any AI consulting work.

BEST QUOTE

"The answers are much better."
