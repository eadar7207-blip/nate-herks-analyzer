## [I Studied 10,000 AI Websites… Here’s what I learned](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mImbbszCjCI)
*November 11 at 01:30 AM*

WHAT IT'S ABOUT

Nate studied over 10,000 websites built with AI (artificial intelligence, meaning computer programs that can generate web pages for you) to figure out why some work and most don't. He found that AI tools make sites that look okay but fail to turn visitors into customers. He made this video to share the five repeating patterns that separate a cheap-looking AI site from one you could actually sell to a business for $1,000, and to show you exactly how to apply each one.

THE KEY POINTS

- **The "mirror test" means that when the right person lands on your page, the words should instantly reflect their problem and world so they feel "this was made for me."** AI writes generic text, so even a pretty site won't convert well without this.
- A real example: TruckersReport (a job site for truck drivers) rewrote its page around what drivers actually needed and got a **79.3% jump in conversions** (the share of visitors who take the action you want).
- Your first screen must answer three questions: **who is this for, what will I get, and what do I do next** — with one single call-to-action button (the button that tells people exactly what to do, like "book a free call").
- Talk about results, not features — say "fill your calendar without answering messages" instead of "AI-powered scheduling." Small word changes matter: one site swapped a word to match what people type into Google and got **31.4% more signups**.
- **The "marker rule" says pick ONE bright accent color and use it only for things people should click.** AI slaps color everywhere, which makes nothing stand out and looks cheap.
- Use the designer's **60-30-10 rule**: 60% main color (background), 30% secondary (text, menus, cards), 10% accent (buttons and links only).
- **The "foreground, middle, background" pattern gives a site depth so the eye knows where to look, which makes it feel premium instead of flat.** On cheap AI sites everything sits on one flat level.

THE METHOD OR FRAMEWORK

Nate's five-pattern system (he fully covers three before the transcript cuts off):

1. **Mirror Test (the words):** Make the first screen reflect the visitor back to themselves. Answer three questions in your text — who it's for, what they get (focus on the result), and what to do next (one clear button). Write these answers yourself first, then paste them into Claude (an AI chat tool) before asking it to design anything.

2. **Marker Rule (the color):** Choose one standout accent color and reserve it only for clickable things. Follow the 60-30-10 split. You can grab a color palette from a site like Coolors, paste the color codes into Claude, and tell it to follow 60-30-10.

3. **Foreground, Middle, Background (the depth):** Build your page in three layers like a theater stage. Step one — put something real in the background (a photo or soft shape instead of a plain white block). Step two — let one element spill over the edge into the next section so it doesn't look like stacked boxes. Step three — add a soft shadow to your main button or card so it clearly pops forward.

HOW THIS APPLIES TO AI REAL ESTATE

If you run an AI real estate consulting business, your own website is your first sales pitch, and these patterns decide whether a real estate agent or broker trusts you enough to book a call. Say you're targeting busy agents who hate chasing cold leads. Instead of a generic headline like "AI automation solutions," you'd use the mirror test and write "Stop losing leads while you're at showings — let AI follow up for you," so the agent instantly sees their own pain. You'd keep the whole site black, white, and gray, with one orange button — "Book Your Free Demo" — as the only accent color, using the marker rule so it's impossible to miss. Then you'd add depth: a soft background photo of a sold-home sign, a card showing a sample automated text conversation spilling slightly over the section edge, and a shadowed button floating in front. That site feels custom-built and expensive, which is exactly what justifies charging a client $1,000-plus.

ACTION STEP THIS WEEK

Rewrite the top section (first screen) of your own site this week. Open a document and write three clear answers: (1) exactly who it's for, (2) the single biggest result you deliver stated as an outcome not a feature, and (3) one call-to-action button label. Paste those three answers into Claude and ask it to rebuild your hero section around them. Then open your site and check your accent color — if it appears anywhere besides clickable buttons and links, strip it out so you follow the 60-30-10 rule.

BEST QUOTE

"When the right visitor lands on your first screen, they should feel like they're looking in a mirror."

## [Every AI Business Ranked from Worst to Best](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O3g_m2i2IGU)
*November 11 at 01:30 AM*

WHAT IT'S ABOUT

Nate made this video to help people cut through the noise and figure out which AI-based business is actually worth starting. There are tons of options popping up every day, and most beginners waste time picking the wrong one. So Nate ranks 13 different AI businesses from worst to best, judging each on three things: how much technical skill you need, whether there are enough buyers right now, and whether the business can last long-term. The goal is to save you from chasing hype and point you toward something solid.

THE KEY POINTS

- **Every business on the list can actually work — the real question is which problems you're willing to deal with.** It's subjective, based on Nate's own experience and what he's seen in his community.
- He scores each one on three factors: technical complexity (how much skill it takes to make something people pay for), market (whether there are more buyers than sellers right now), and sustainability (whether it can grow into a lasting business).
- **AI SaaS (building software with AI tools like Claude Code and charging a monthly fee) ranks dead last at #13 — not because it's bad, but because it's the hardest to get off the ground.** Building the app is easy; getting people to find it, pay, and stay is the real battle, and it usually only works for people who already have an audience or cash flow.
- Faceless AI channels (#12) — social media accounts where AI makes the content and you never show your face — can work, but **views alone don't make money; you need an audience that sponsors actually want to reach.** Platforms like YouTube won't pay for mass-produced AI junk.
- **AI websites (#11) are where Nate tells complete beginners to start** — you can build a client website in an evening with tools like Lovable, undercutting agencies that charge $5,000–$10,000. But it has a low income ceiling (around $10K–$20K a month) and cheap subscription builders are already eating into it.
- AI Operating Systems (#10) — one central hub where a company's AI agents, tools, and knowledge live — make a good "foot in the door" offer, but **it's hard to tie them to a clear business result the client cares about,** and they only work if the owner already believes in AI.
- GEO (#9), or optimizing a brand to get recommended by AI chatbots like ChatGPT — is like SEO but for AI, and **huge money is flowing in while almost nobody in Nate's community is talking about it yet.** It's a strong fit if you already know SEO, but tough without that background.
- AI receptionists and support agents (#8) — AI that answers calls, chats, and emails for businesses — have an **extremely easy-to-explain payoff because a missed call is literally lost money** (one study found only 56% of 70 million business calls got answered by a human). The weakness is that big companies are now building this feature in themselves.

THE METHOD OR FRAMEWORK

Nate uses a simple three-part scoring system to judge each AI business. Here it is in plain English:

1. **Technical complexity** — How much skill do you need to build something people will actually pay for? Important twist: a LOW score here means you need MORE skill (so lower is harder). For example, AI SaaS scored a 4, meaning it's relatively doable, but security and handling thousands of users makes it genuinely hard.
2. **Market** — Are there currently more people wanting to buy than people selling? A higher score means it's a better time to get in.
3. **Sustainability** — Can this turn into a real, lasting business that keeps growing, or will one platform update wipe you out overnight?

He gives each business a rating on all three and ranks them worst to best based on the overall picture. A recurring theme: **every good offer should have one clear metric tied to a business outcome the client cares about** (like money saved or leads captured). If you can't point to a clear result, the business is weaker.

HOW THIS APPLIES TO AI REAL ESTATE

The two businesses Nate ranks highest in this clip — AI receptionists/support agents and GEO — map almost perfectly onto real estate. Real estate agents and brokerages live and die by lead response speed, and just like dentists, a missed call is a lost deal. Here's a concrete scenario: imagine a busy real estate team that gets 40 inbound calls and web inquiries a day but only answers about half because agents are out at showings. You set up an AI receptionist that answers every call and website chat 24/7, qualifies the lead (budget, location, timeline), books the showing on the agent's calendar, and hands off to a human when needed. You charge a $2,500 setup fee plus a monthly retainer. The ROI pitch is dead simple: "You're currently missing half your calls — if each closed deal is worth $10,000 in commission, catching even two extra deals a year pays for this many times over." That's a crystal-clear business metric, which is exactly what Nate says every strong offer needs.

(2) Use a no-code AI agent tool to create a simple AI that answers a call or web chat, asks three qualifying questions (budget, area, timeline), and books a showing time. (3) Record a 2-minute screen video of it working. (4) Message five local agents offering to show them the demo. Having something they can see and hear is what turns a vague pitch into a paying client.

"Every offer you make should have one clear metric that you are influencing, and it should be a business outcome that they care about."

## [Every Claude 2.0 Concept Explained for Normal People](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tcb5q_6jwvY)
*November 11 at 01:30 AM*

WHAT IT'S ABOUT

Nate made this video to clear up the confusion around what people are calling "Claude 2.0" — a big batch of updates that Anthropic (the company that makes the AI assistant called Claude) rolled out over six weeks. There's no single new product called "Claude 2.0," it's just a nickname for all the new stuff. So Nate walks through 25 key ideas in plain language, grouped into five parts, so a normal person who doesn't code or work in tech can understand what changed and how to actually use it.

THE KEY POINTS

- **Claude isn't one AI, it's a family of models (different versions you can pick from).** The three main ones are Haiku (smallest, cheapest, fastest), Sonnet (the reliable everyday "workhorse"), and Opus (for big, complex jobs).
- There's also a top-tier model called Fable 5.1 that's extremely powerful and expensive — **so powerful the government restricted the earlier Fable 5 version** — but Nate says Opus does almost as well on most tasks for far less money.
- **Sonnet beats Opus when instructions are clear, and Opus beats Sonnet when the goal is vague** (like building a 90-day plan). Treat the models like a team — don't give every small task to your most experienced worker, and don't hand a big project to a beginner.
- Tokens (small chunks of words the AI counts) are how everything gets measured — both your cost and your limits. The "context window" (how much the AI can hold in memory at once) is 1 million tokens, roughly 555,000 words, or a stack of novels.
- **Most of your tokens get used reading files, not writing answers** — Nate measured about 200 reads for every 1 write — so the files and instructions you load matter way more than how long your question is.
- "Effort" means how hard Claude thinks before answering (low to maximum). **More effort isn't always better — Nate's test showed "Max" cost $50, took 2.5 hours, and still made mistakes.** He uses medium daily and bumps it up only for complex work.
- Claude has two limits running at once: a 5-hour session limit and a weekly limit, and they're shared across the app and Claude Code. **The $200 "Ultra" plan now removes the 5-hour limit.**
- The app now has three tabs: Chat (quick writing, searching, brainstorming), Code (a real assistant that works on files for you), and Duet (the new agent app).
- **Claude Code isn't just for programmers** — Nate runs almost his entire business through it in one folder, and nobody on his team is a professional coder.

THE METHOD OR FRAMEWORK

Nate organizes the 25 Claude concepts into five parts so they're easier to digest:

1. **The "Brain"** — what Claude actually runs on: the models (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, Fable), tokens and context window (how memory and cost are measured), effort levels (how hard it thinks), and usage limits/plans.
2. **Where you use it** — the three tabs in the app: Chat, Code, and Duet.

His practical model-picking rule: match the model to the job. Use Sonnet when your instructions are clear and specific, use Opus when the goal is fuzzy and needs more reasoning, and only reach for the expensive Fable for the hardest, longest, independent tasks. For effort, default to medium and only raise it for genuinely complex work like video editing. There's also a "/advisor" command that lets a cheaper model call in a more powerful one (like Fable) only at the key moments, so you get extra brainpower without paying for it the whole time.

HOW THIS APPLIES TO AI REAL ESTATE

The model-matching lesson maps directly onto running an AI real estate consulting business, where your profit depends on not overspending on AI for simple jobs. For example, if a client wants you to auto-generate 50 standardized property listing descriptions from a spreadsheet — that's a clear, well-defined task — you'd run it on Sonnet at medium effort and keep costs low. But if a client asks you to build a vague, open-ended deliverable like "a 90-day marketing strategy to break into a new neighborhood," that's where you'd switch to Opus, because fuzzy goals need deeper reasoning. Knowing that files you load eat up 200 times more tokens than your actual request also matters: if you dump every MLS record and contract into one prompt, you'll burn through your limits fast, so you'd feed the AI only the relevant files. Using Claude Code (the assistant that works inside a folder on your computer) means you could build a single folder for each client with their listings, contracts, and templates, and have the AI draft documents, update spreadsheets, and organize files automatically.

ACTION STEP THIS WEEK

Run Nate's exact model test on your own real estate workflow. Take one clear task (like writing 10 listing descriptions) and one vague task (like outlining a lead-generation plan for a new area). Run the clear task on Sonnet at medium effort, and run the vague task on Opus. Type /usage after each to see what you spent, and write down which model gave the better result for each type of job. By the end of the week you'll have your own cheat sheet for which model to use when, instead of guessing.

BEST QUOTE

"It's literally like having a best friend who is the coolest programmer in the world and communicates with you like a norm[al person]."

## [4 Claude Systems That Sell For $3000+ Right Now (With PROOF)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQJRLZ2q_Ns)
*November 11 at 01:30 AM*

WHAT IT'S ABOUT

This video is Nate Herk showing four real businesses — what he calls "systems" — that people in his online community built using Claude (an AI tool similar to ChatGPT that can read, write, and organize information). Each of these was actually sold to a real client for thousands of dollars. Nate made this video to prove that regular people with basic AI skills can build and sell these automations, and to show you exactly what the systems do, why clients happily pay for them, and how you can pitch and sell the same thing yourself.

THE KEY POINTS

- **The four systems together sold for about $3,700 each on average, and every one was built by an ordinary community member — not a tech genius.** This proves the barrier to entry is low.
- **System 1 turns voice messages into finished documents.** A worker records a quick voice note, and the AI turns it into a polished, branded PDF (a professional-looking file) that saves and sends itself automatically.
- **You sell these systems by talking about saved time and reduced risk, not the technology.** For example, 5 workers wasting 5 hours each a week on paperwork equals 25 hours you can give back to the business.
- **System 2 is a "funnel" — a step-by-step path that carries a stranger from a social media post all the way to a paid booking, with no chasing from the owner.** It posts content, captures leads, texts and emails payment links, sends forms, and books Zoom calls automatically.
- **Sell System 2 by tying it directly to money.** If 40 people fill out a form but only 10 pay, this system "pushes" the other 30 toward paying — and a few extra sales easily covers the cost.
- **System 3 is "Creator OS," an operating system for content creators that makes videos and images in the creator's own cloned voice and face.** This means they produce content without filming or writing anything.
- **The biggest wins came with a recurring monthly fee on top of the one-time build price.** One builder got $3,600 upfront plus $197 every month afterward.
- **Across all systems, the golden rule is: sell the business result (more sales, saved hours), never the technical features.** Owners don't care how it's built — they care what it does for them.

THE METHOD OR FRAMEWORK

Nate gives a repeatable way to find clients and sell each system:

1. **Pick the right type of business for each system.** For the document system, choose industries where people don't sit at desks — construction, plumbing, electrical. For the funnel, choose booking-based businesses where leads come in but nobody follows up.
2. **Find their biggest pain.** Ask the owner which document their team hates most, or how many people filled out a form last month versus how many actually paid.
3. **Put a real dollar number on the problem.** Multiply wasted hours by number of workers, or count the lost sales, so the price feels obviously worth it.
4. **Map their process on one page.** Mark the steps where a human is truly needed, and automate everything in between.
5. **Record a short demo video and send it.** This proves it works before they pay.
6. **Start small, then expand.** Once one document or one funnel works, add the next one and keep automating their routine for ongoing income.

HOW THIS APPLIES TO AI REAL ESTATE

The funnel system (System 2) maps almost perfectly onto a real estate business. Picture a busy real estate agent who gets plenty of leads from Facebook and Instagram posts but loses most of them because they're too busy showing houses to follow up. You could build them a system that automatically posts listings each week, captures anyone who fills out an "I'm interested" form, instantly texts and emails them, books a showing or consultation call on the agent's calendar, and sends reminders so they actually show up. You'd sell it by asking, "How many leads came in last month, and how many did you actually close?" If 50 leads came in and only 5 closed, and each closed deal earns the agent a $9,000 commission, then catching even two or three more deals a month makes a $2,200 build fee a no-brainer — plus a monthly fee to keep the content posting.

Then take their answer, do the math on how many deals they're leaving on the table, and record a quick 5-minute screen-recording video showing a simple follow-up funnel that would catch those lost leads. Send that video to them. That's it — one real agent, two questions, one demo video, this week.
